## 1. The polycrisis literature: Lawrence, Homer-Dixon et al. 2024

**Citation (verified from the PDF's own title page):** Lawrence M, Homer-Dixon T, Janzwood S, Rockström J, Renn O, Donges JF (2024). "Global polycrisis: the causal mechanisms of crisis entanglement." *Global Sustainability* 7, e6, 1–16. https://doi.org/10.1017/sus.2024.1 (Open Access, CC-BY 4.0. Received 20 June 2023; accepted 14 January 2024. Note there is a published erratum, DOI 10.1017/sus.2024.48, correcting a spelling in the author list.)

**Definition (verbatim, p.4):** a global polycrisis is *"the causal entanglement of crises in multiple global systems in ways that significantly degrade humanity's prospects."*

**The core generative model (verbatim, abstract):** *"a global crisis arises when one or more fast-moving trigger events combine with slow-moving stresses to push a global system out of its established equilibrium and into a volatile and harmful state of disequilibrium."* This is the single most simulator-relevant sentence in the paper: it says a hazard model needs **two state variables per system** — a slow-moving stress level (which reshapes the stability landscape) and a stochastic fast trigger — and that crisis probability is *multiplicative* in them. Their Figure 4a is explicit: *"The multiplication sign indicates that stresses and trigger are both causally necessary for the crisis outcome and that the trigger multiplies the impact of the underlying stresses."*

**Their taxonomy of causal mechanisms.** Two layers, and a simulator should encode both.

*Layer 1 — three causal pathways* (adapted, they say explicitly, from Rocha et al. 2018's Regime Shifts Database; they renamed "shared drivers" → "common stresses" and "hidden feedbacks" → "inter-systemic feedbacks"), verbatim from §4.2, p.9:

| Pathway | Verbatim definition |
|---|---|
| **Common stresses** | *"A common stress may weaken the resilience of multiple systems, or the stresses affecting one system may interact with stresses in another"* |
| **Domino effects** | *"A crisis in one system may affect the stresses in another system, cause a triggering event that pushes another system into crisis, or cause a crisis in another system… Domino effects operate in temporal sequence."* |
| **Inter-systemic feedbacks** | *"Stresses, trigger events, and other events generated by a crisis can form feedback loops that either dampen or, more commonly, escalate crises in two or more systems."* |

*Layer 2 — six "ideal types" of inter-system interaction* (Figure 6a–f, p.8). These are the actual edge types a simulator should support, and they are more useful than the three pathways because they distinguish *what gets modified*:

- **6a Common stresses** — one stress raises the stress level in ≥2 systems.
- **6b Common triggers** — one trigger event fires into ≥2 systems' crisis conditions.
- **6c Interacting stresses** — a stress in system A causally raises a stress in system B (they give: food insecurity → forced reallocation of household income away from education/investment → greater poverty and inequality).
- **6d Inter-systemic stress–trigger interactions** — a stress in A *generates a trigger event* in B (they give: climate heating → habitat disruption → expanded human/novel-animal contact zone → raised likelihood of zoonotic spillover).
- **6e Crisis impacts on adjacent systems** — a crisis in A modifies the stresses and/or triggers of B (they give: COVID-19 deepened socio-economic inequality; fiscal response worsened inflation).
- **6f Inter-systemic crisis interactions** — a crisis in A interacts with a crisis in B, *altering the dynamics of each* (they give: an international security crisis worsens the climate crisis by diverting attention and resources; the climate crisis intensifies the security crisis via conflict over resources and mass migration).

They also give **five properties of global systems that enable polycrises** (Box 2, p.4) — these are effectively the modelling requirements: *multiple causes* (synergistic, non-additive), *non-linearity* (multiple equilibria, thresholds, tipping), *hysteresis* (*"System flips are generally not reversible; a return to the previous system equilibrium is often impossible"*), *boundary permeability*, and *"black swan" outcomes* (*"probability density functions describing the distribution of events generated by complex global systems are rarely normal (i.e. Gaussian); they often have long tails"*). **Simulator implication: fat-tailed severity draws and irreversible state changes are not optional realism, they are the mechanism.**

Their eight global systems (Figure 1) are a ready-made node set: **Economy, Health, Social Order & Governance, Food, International Security, Energy, Environment, Transportation & Communication.** Four *vectors* carry crises between them (Box 1): **energy, matter, information, biota.** The *conduits* are telecom networks, pipelines and electrical grids, roads/canals/air and shipping routes, supply chains, trade, finance and monetary systems, and the links among climate and ecological systems.

**Their worked domino chain (Figure 8, p.10) is itself an adjacency list**, and I reproduce its edges below in §6 because it is the closest thing in the literature to a hand-drawn causal graph of the present polycrisis. Their causal-loop diagrams (Figure 9a–d, p.11) give four *feedback* loops: economic turmoil ↔ populist authoritarianism ↔ nationalist/anti-globalisation ideology; that triad → decline of international cooperation; decline of cooperation → weakening of the international climate regime → extreme weather → mass migration → chauvinist reaction → economic turmoil; and finally civil violence & interstate war closing the loop back into migration and economic turmoil.

**One quantitative interconnection datum they give (p.5, sourced to World Bank DataBank and UNCTADstat):** between 1980 and 2020 *"air freight increased sixfold to 180 billion ton-kilometers per year, the number of air passengers nearly tripled to 1.8 billion, and internet usage increased from virtually 0 to 60% of the world's population… the total value of world merchandise trade increased twelve-fold between 1980 and 2022 to nearly 25 trillion US dollars annually (at current prices), and container port traffic has more than tripled since 2000 to almost 800 million 20-foot-equivalent-units in 2020."* This is the coupling-strength trend term: **edge weights in this graph are not constant over time; they have been rising monotonically for four decades.**

**Their own methodological critique of the incumbent (WEF Global Risks Report) network maps, p.12 — read this as a spec for what your simulator must do better:** the WEF diagrams *"depict only positive correlations between risks — that is, about the likelihood that certain risks will appear together — not actual causal connections between them"*; they omit negative correlations; they are *"a static snapshot of risk connections at a particular moment"* and *"do not reveal how risks and their connections change over time as crises occur and activate other risks"*; and their data comes from surveys of business leaders, whose risk judgements are systematically *less* severe than scientists'. A simulator with genuine directed causal edges, sign, and time-varying activation is a strict improvement on the field's most-cited artefact.

## 2. Cambridge CSER / Global Challenges Foundation on risk interaction

**Avin S, Wintle BC, Weitzdörfer J, Ó hÉigeartaigh SS, Sutherland WJ, Rees MJ (2018). "Classifying global catastrophic risks." *Futures* 102: 20–26.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.futures.2018.02.001 (CC-BY; I read the accepted PDF from the Cambridge repository.)

This is the best available *structural* decomposition for a simulator, because it refuses to model risks as monolithic events. Verbatim from §1: analysis breaks into *"(i) a critical system (or systems) whose safety boundaries are breached by a potential threat, (ii) the mechanisms by which this threat might spread globally and affect the majority of the human population, and (iii) the manner in which we might fail to prevent or mitigate both (i) and (ii)."*

- **Critical systems** (Fig. 1) — defined as *"any system or process that, if disturbed beyond a certain limit or scale, could trigger a significant reduction in humanity's ability to survive in its current form"*, arranged in a hierarchy from lower to higher level: **Physical** (stable space/time, complex organic molecules, viable radiation levels, viable temperature range); **Biogeochemical** (carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorous, sulphur cycles, viable acidity levels, water cycle); **Cellular**; **Anatomical**; **Whole organism**; **Ecological** (decomposition, food chains, mutualism, primary production); **Sociotechnological** (climate control, food, health, security, shelter, utilities, resource extraction). Higher-level systems depend on lower-level ones — *that dependency ordering is itself a coupling structure.*
- **Global spread mechanisms** (Fig. 2): **Natural global scale** (astronomical; air-based dispersal; water-based dispersal); **Anthropogenic networks** (communication; utilities; commerce & transit); **Replicators** (biological; digital; cultural).
- **Prevention & mitigation fragilities** (Fig. 3): **Individual** (cognitive bias, empowerment, motivation, reflection, risk perception, skill, values); **Interpersonal** (communication, conflict resolution, connection, inclusion, reputation, trust); **Institutional** (adaptability, decision making, ethics, incentives, information, innovativeness, management/power structure, participation/transparency, resources, staffing); **Beyond institutional** (compliance/enforcement, coordination/responsibility, diversity, good governance, institutional mix, monitoring/surveillance, representation).

Their Table 1 cross-tabulates eight scenarios (asteroid impact, volcanic super-eruption, natural pandemic, ecosystem collapse, nuclear war, bioengineered pathogen, weaponised AI, geoengineering termination) against critical systems × spread mechanism. **The convergence is the point:** many distinct hazards compromise the *same* critical systems through the *same* conduits. A simulator that models hazards as independent dice on outcomes will systematically understate correlated failure; a simulator that routes every hazard through a shared critical-systems layer gets the correlation structure for free.

**Cotton-Barratt O, Farquhar S, Halstead J, Schubert S, Snyder-Beattie A (2016). *Global Catastrophic Risks 2016.* Global Challenges Foundation / Global Priorities Project & Future of Humanity Institute.** PDF: https://globalchallenges.org//app/uploads/2023/06/Global-Catastrophic-Risks-2016.pdf (read directly).

Their definition, §1.1, verbatim: *"We define a global catastrophe as a possible event or process that, were it to occur, would end the lives of approximately 10% or more of the global population, or do comparable damage. Extinction risks are a subset of global catastrophic risks, which would end the human race."* (This 10% threshold is the one Lawrence et al. adopt; note Kemp et al. use **25%** for the same word — see §3. **Do not conflate them.**)

Chapter 3 is explicitly titled *"Risk factors and interactions between risks"* and states, verbatim: *"Global catastrophic risks are parts of extremely complicated causal networks."* Its §3.2 gives specific couplings I have encoded below, including the crisp statement: *"Greater disasters are likely to lead to even more upheaval, and might significantly weaken the defenses against further catastrophic events. In this way, one global catastrophe might trigger another."* And the shared-mechanism observation that is the single highest-leverage insight for a simulator's mitigation layer: *"Nuclear war, geo-engineering, super-volcanoes, asteroids, and comets all pose global catastrophic risk in significant part because of the 'particulate winter' scenarios they might produce… Because so many risks share this mechanism, many strategies for resilience that address one risk address several. Food stockpiles and the ability to rapidly increase production of alternate sources of food would increase resilience to a broad range of risks."*

Their governance edge, verbatim: *"bad governance, nationally or internationally, could increase the likelihood and potential impact of every risk."* — i.e. governance quality is a **global multiplier node**, not an edge.

## 3. Kemp et al. 2022, "Climate Endgame" — climate as threat multiplier

**Citation:** Kemp L, Xu C, Depledge J, Ebi KL, Gibbins G, Kohler TA, Rockström J, Scheffer M, Schellnhuber HJ, Steffen W, Lenton TM (2022). "Climate Endgame: Exploring catastrophic climate change scenarios." *PNAS* 119(34): e2108146119. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2108146119. PMID 35914185, PMCID PMC9407216. (pnas.org 403s; I read the PMC full text.)

**The four horsemen, verbatim:** *"The 'four horsemen' of the climate change end game are likely to be famine and undernutrition, extreme weather events, conflict, and vector-borne diseases."*

**The multiplier claim, verbatim:** *"climate change could directly trigger other catastrophic risks, such as international conflict, or exacerbate infectious disease spread, and spillover risk."* Their framing is that climate is a **potent extreme threat multiplier**, not a stand-alone kill mechanism — which is exactly why their fourth research question is *"How can these multiple strands of evidence — together with other global dangers — be usefully synthesized into an 'integrated catastrophe assessment'?"* Your simulator is, in effect, an attempt at that.

**Their definitions (Table 1) — and note carefully that these are the three-way distinction the brief demands:**

| Term | Verbatim definition |
|---|---|
| Global catastrophic risk | *"The probability of a loss of 25% of the global population and the severe disruption of global critical systems (such as food) within a given timeframe (years or decades)."* |
| Global decimation risk | *"The probability of a loss of 10% (or more) of global population and the severe disruption of global critical systems (such as food) within a given timeframe (years or decades)."* |
| Extinction risk | *"The probability of human extinction within a given timeframe."* |
| Systemic risk | *"Potential for individual disruptions or failures to cascade into a system-wide failure"* |
| Risk cascade | *"Chains of risk occurring when an adverse impact triggers linked risks"* |

**Quantitative anchors retrieved:** *"there remains an 18% probability that ECS could be greater than 4.5 °C"*; and *"by 2070, around 2 billion people are expected to live in these extremely hot areas"* (areas of mean annual temperature >29 °C), under *"SSP3-7.0 emissions, and SSP3 population growth"* — against roughly 30 million today. Their named cascade mechanisms include tipping-element interaction (triggering one raises the probability of others), infrastructure failure enabling a subsequent hazard (a cyclone destroying electrical systems immediately before a heat wave), food-system failure propagating into conflict and migration, and systemic financial contagion.

## 4. Documented historical cascades — physical shock into social catastrophe

### 4.1 Laki, 1783–84 → European mortality

**Thordarson T, Self S (2003). "Atmospheric and environmental effects of the 1783–1784 Laki eruption: A review and reassessment." *J. Geophys. Res. Atmospheres* 108. https://doi.org/10.1029/2001jd002042** — the standard reassessment (metadata retrieved via Crossref; I did not obtain the full text).

**Balkanski Y, Menut L, Garnier E, Wang R, Evangeliou N, Jourdain S, Eschstruth C, Vrac M, Yiou P (2018). "Mortality induced by PM₂.₅ exposure following the 1783 Laki eruption using reconstructed meteorological fields." *Scientific Reports* 8(1): 15896. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-34228-7. PMID 30367172, PMCID PMC6203706.**

Retrieved figures, as the source states them: in French parish records, *"4193 individuals deceased in JJAS 1783, a 32% higher number than the average of 3092 deceased for the period 1774–1789"*, and *"The deviation from the mean of the summer 1783 anomaly of deceased is 3 times greater than any other year of that period."* Modelled concentrations: *"SO2 concentrations exceed 30 μg m−3 off the coast of Norway"* and *"PM2.5 boundary layer concentrations range between 25 and 30 μg m−3 over a broad band that goes from SW France to the Netherlands."* For Iceland: *"The excess SO2 from the Laki eruption increased from 13 to 30% the death rate of the Icelandic population."* **Caution:** that last sentence, as retrieved, is syntactically ambiguous (a range 13–30%? an increase from 13% to 30%?) and I could not disambiguate it against the figure captions; treat as `13–30% Icelandic death rate, exact reading unresolved`. The paper's own conclusion is that excess mortality *"involved multiple factors beyond modern PM₂.₅ relationships, including summer heat and limited healthcare availability"* — i.e. **the aerosol edge and the heat edge were super-additive, and the mitigation-capacity node modulated both.** That is a three-way interaction, not a dose-response curve.

Also retrieved: Casey JA, Gemmill A, Elser H, Karasek D, Catalano R (2019), "Sun Smoke in Sweden: Perinatal Implications of the Laki Volcanic Eruptions, 1783-1784," *Epidemiology* 30(3): 330–333, https://doi.org/10.1097/ede.0000000000000977 — *"the secondary sex ratio decreased 3% below expected levels in 1784"* and infant mortality *"exceeded expectation in 1785, by 54% (95% CI = 25%, 83%) and 37%"* (female and male respectively). Note the **two-year lag** to the infant-mortality peak: the demographic damage function is not synchronous with the forcing.

### 4.2 Tambora 1815 → 1816 Year Without a Summer → subsistence crisis and typhus

**Oppenheimer C (2003). "Climatic, environmental and human consequences of the largest known historic eruption: Tambora volcano (Indonesia) 1815." *Progress in Physical Geography* 27(2): 230–259. https://doi.org/10.1191/0309133303pp379ra** (closed access; abstract reconstructed via OpenAlex). Figures as stated: ~140 gigatons of magma expelled; plumes to 43 km; **over 71,000 deaths on Sumbawa and neighbouring Lombok**; approximately **60 megatons of sulfur** into the stratosphere, **six times more than 1991 Pinatubo**; the aerosol veil caused anomalously cold weather in the northeastern USA, maritime Canada and Europe; *"Crop failures and epidemic typhus outbreaks followed."*

Cross-check on the sulfur mass — **Self S, Gertisser R, Thordarson T, Rampino MR, Wolff JA (2004), "Magma volume, volatile emissions, and stratospheric aerosols from the 1815 eruption of Tambora," *Geophys. Res. Lett.* 31(20), https://doi.org/10.1029/2004GL020925** — gives a *smaller* magma volume (30–33 km³) and **53–58 Tg SO₂ released over ~24 hours on 10–11 April 1815, generating 93–118 Tg of stratospheric sulfate aerosol.** The two sources disagree on magma volume; report the range, not a point.

**The attribution multiplier — this is the best single number in the historical-cascade literature.** Schurer AP, Hegerl GC, Luterbacher J, Brönnimann S, Cowan T, Tett SFB, Zanchettin D, Timmreck C (2019). "Disentangling the causes of the 1816 European year without a summer." *Environmental Research Letters* 14(9): 094019. https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/ab3a10 (open access; IOP is bot-walled, metadata + abstract via OpenAlex). As stated: circulation patterns *"account for only about one-quarter of the observed cold conditions"*; **the Tambora eruption made cold summers approximately 100 times more likely and precipitation anomalies 1.5–3 times more probable.** That is a probability-ratio edge in exactly the form a simulator wants: `volcanic_stratospheric_aerosol → regional_cold_summer, P-ratio ≈ 100×; → wet_anomaly, P-ratio 1.5–3×`.

Also retrieved: Luterbacher J & Pfister C (2015), "The year without a summer," *Nature Geoscience* 8: 246–248, https://doi.org/10.1038/ngeo2404 (metadata only, paywalled).

**The typhus link is the weak leg of this chain in my retrieved evidence.** I have Oppenheimer's assertion that epidemic typhus followed, but I could **not** retrieve a quantitative epidemiological study of the 1816–19 European/Irish typhus epidemic this session (my Crossref queries returned only tangential 19th- and 20th-century typhus material). The canonical secondary source is John D. Post's *The Last Great Subsistence Crisis in the Western World* (1977) — **I did not retrieve it and am not citing a figure from it.** Any typhus mortality number in the simulator must be marked `AUTHOR-ESTIMATE` or sourced properly later.

### 4.3 The 1876–78 El Niño famines

**Singh D, Seager R, Cook BI, Cane M, Ting M, Cook E, Davis M (2018). "Climate and the Global Famine of 1876–78." *Journal of Climate* 31(23): 9445–9467. https://doi.org/10.1175/jcli-d-18-0159.1** (AMS 403s to fetches; abstract reconstructed via OpenAlex). As stated: concurrent multiyear droughts across Asia, Brazil and Africa 1875–1878 — the "Great Drought" — triggering the Global Famine with **over 50 million fatalities**; "Monsoon Asia" experienced **the most intense drought in 800 years**; the forcing sequence was cool tropical Pacific conditions (1870–76), a record El Niño (1877–78), a strong Indian Ocean Dipole (1877), and warm North Atlantic conditions (1878); tropical Pacific SSTs were largely responsible for the monsoon-region impacts; and the conditions *"arose from natural variability, though future recurrence under warming could threaten global food security."*

**On the contested politics.** Note that **Mike Davis is a co-author of this paper** — the climate-attribution community and Davis's political-economy thesis are not, in 2018, in opposition. Davis's *Late Victorian Holocausts: El Niño Famines and the Making of the Third World* (Verso, 2001) argues the deaths were produced by the *interaction* of ENSO drought with colonial market integration, grain export policy, and the deliberate withholding of relief — not by the drought alone. That thesis is genuinely contested; I retrieved seven review citations attesting to the debate but did not read them, so I record their existence rather than their content: Epstein J, *Victorian Studies* 45 (2003) 529–530, DOI 10.1353/vic.2003.0118; Jones T, *Technology and Culture* 43 (2002) 463–464, DOI 10.1353/tech.2002.0066; Cottrell C, *Journal of World History* 14 (2003) 577–579, DOI 10.1353/jwh.2003.0050; Satya LD, *Environment and History* 9 (2003) 239–240, DOI 10.3828/whp.eh.63826917592084; New M, *The Holocene* 13 (2003) 148, DOI 10.1177/095968360301300118; Leitch R, *Review of Radical Political Economics* 40 (2008) 384–387, DOI 10.1177/0486613408319853. **Simulator implication regardless of where one lands: the drought→famine edge weight is not a physical constant. It is set by a policy/institutional node (relief capacity, export policy, market integration), and the same rainfall deficit produces radically different mortality depending on that node's state.** This is the same structure Balkanski et al. found for Laki and the same structure Hendrix & Haggard found for food riots (§4.5). It recurs three times independently in this brief; encode it as a first-class mechanic.

### 4.4 The 1930s Dust Bowl — a land-surface feedback that amplified its own drought

**Cook BI, Miller RL, Seager R (2009). "Amplification of the North American 'Dust Bowl' drought through human-induced land degradation." *PNAS* 106: 4997–5001. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0810200106** (abstract reconstructed via OpenAlex; PMC copy exists at PMC2664008). As stated: GCMs forced by 1930s SSTs alone produce a drought centred on the *southwestern* US with **no** mid-continent warming — i.e. **the ocean forcing alone does not reproduce the event.** Adding human land degradation (vegetation reduction and soil-dust aerosol sources, both consequences of crop failure) is *necessary*: vegetation reduction explains the high temperature anomalies over the US, aerosols intensify the drought and shift it northward, and with both included the model precipitation matches observations in magnitude and location. Their conclusion: land degradation *"likely amplified a modest ocean-forced drought into one of the worst environmental disasters the U.S. has experienced."*

**This is the cleanest documented case of a *positive feedback from the social response back onto the physical hazard*** — crop failure → land degradation → more dust and less vegetation → hotter, drier, further north → more crop failure. Most cascade models are unidirectional (physical → social). This one closes the loop. Companion: Cook, Miller & Seager (2008), *GRL* 35, DOI 10.1029/2008gl033486; Cook, Seager, Miller & Mason (2013), *J. Climate* 26: 4414–4430, DOI 10.1175/jcli-d-12-00022.1.

### 4.5 2010 Russian heatwave → export ban → 2011 price spike → Arab Spring

**The heat–smoke interaction (a rare *measured* super-additive compound hazard).** Shaposhnikov D, Revich B, Bellander T, Bedada GB, Bottai M, Kharkova T, Kvasha E, Lezina E, Lind T, Semutnikova E, Pershagen G (2014). "Mortality related to air pollution with the Moscow heat wave and wildfire of 2010." *Epidemiology* 25(3): 359–364. https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0000000000000090. As stated: the 44-day heat wave produced *"close to 11,000 excess deaths from nonaccidental causes during this period, mainly among those older than 65 years"*, and — critically — *"interactions between high temperatures and wildfire air pollution in excess of an additive effect contributed to more than 2000 deaths."* **≈18% of the death toll came from the interaction term alone.** Also retrieved: Zemtsov S et al. (2020), *Health & Place* 66: 102429, DOI 10.1016/j.healthplace.2020.102429, stating Moscow's 2010 heat wave *"resulted in more than 10,000 extra deaths and significant economic damage."*

**The export-ban leg.** Götz L, Glauben T, Brümmer B (2013). "Wheat export restrictions and domestic market effects in Russia and Ukraine during the food crisis." *Food Policy* 38: 214–226. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodpol.2012.12.001. **I retrieved metadata only** — Elsevier closed access, OpenAlex reports no abstract and no OA copy. **I therefore have no retrieved number for the size of the price effect of the 2010 ban and will not invent one.** Related retrieved: Djuric I, Götz L, Glauben T (2014), *Agribusiness* 31: 215–228, DOI 10.1002/agr.21398; Rude J & An H (2015), "Explaining grain and oilseed price volatility: The role of export restrictions," *Food Policy* 57: 83–92, DOI 10.1016/j.foodpol.2015.09.002. The heatwave→export-ban→world-price edge should be marked `MECHANISM PUBLISHED, MAGNITUDE NOT RETRIEVED`.

**The price→unrest leg.** Lagi M, Bertrand KZ, Bar-Yam Y (2011). "The Food Crises and Political Instability in North Africa and the Middle East." arXiv:1108.2455 (dated 19 July 2011, revised 10 August 2011; New England Complex Systems Institute). I read the PDF directly. The threshold, verbatim from p.4: *"food riots occur above a threshold of the FAO price index of 210 (p < 10⁻⁷, binomial test)."* And on the specific 2011 events: *"the probability that the unrest in North Africa and the Middle East occurred by chance at a period of high food prices is p < 0.06 (one sample binomial test)."* Their Figure 1 annotates riots with death tolls in parentheses; retrieved values include Egypt (800+), Libya (10000+), Tunisia (300+), Syria (900+), Yemen (300+), Bahrain (31), Iraq (29), Cameroon (40), Mozambique (13), Somalia (5), Uganda (5), Algeria (4), Sudan (3), Morocco (5), Oman (2), Mauritania (1), Saudi Arabia (1), Burundi (1). Context they give: *"In 2008 more than 60 food riots occurred worldwide in 30 different countries, 10 of which resulted in multiple deaths."*

**The critiques — and they matter for how you encode the edge, not merely as a caveat.**

- **Hendrix CS & Haggard S (2015). "Global food prices, regime type, and urban unrest in the developing world." *Journal of Peace Research* 52(2): 143–157. https://doi.org/10.1177/0022343314561599.** From a dataset of urban unrest in 55 major cities across 49 Asian and African countries, 1961–2010, they find, verbatim from the abstract: *"the effect of global food prices on protests and rioting is contingent on regime type: democracies are more prone to urban unrest during periods of high food prices than autocracies."* This **directly contradicts a single global scalar threshold** and demands the edge be conditioned on a regime-type node. Note also that the specific 2011 cases (Tunisia, Egypt, Libya, Syria) were autocracies — the Hendrix–Haggard result is not a straightforward refutation of Lagi et al.'s case selection, but it does say the coefficient is heterogeneous, and Lagi et al.'s own p<0.06 on the 2011 cluster is not a strong significance level.
- The **climate→conflict** effect size Lagi et al.'s reading depends on is itself contested. **Hsiang SM, Burke M, Miguel E (2013). "Quantifying the Influence of Climate on Human Conflict." *Science* 341(6151): 1235367. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1235367** (CC-BY; I read the eScholarship copy, https://escholarship.org/uc/item/28c3c631). Verbatim from the abstract: *"for each 1 standard deviation (1σ) change in climate towards warmer temperatures or more extreme rainfall, median estimates indicate that the frequency of interpersonal violence rises 4% and the frequency of intergroup conflict rises 14%. Because locations throughout the inhabited world are expected to warm 2-4σ by 2050, amplified rates of human conflict could represent a large and critical impact of anthropogenic climate change."* Their synthesis covers 60 studies.
- **Against it: Buhaug H, Nordkvelle J, Bernauer T, Böhmelt T, Brzoska M, Busby JW, Ciccone A, Fjelde H, Gartzke E, Gleditsch NP, Goldstone JA, Hegre H, Holtermann H, Koubi V, Link JSA, Link PM, Lujala P, O'Loughlin J, Raleigh C, Scheffran J, Schilling J, Smith TG, Theisen OM, Tol RSJ, Urdal H, von Uexkull N (2014). "One effect to rule them all? A comment on climate and conflict." *Climatic Change* 127(3–4): 391–397. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10584-014-1266-1.** Twenty-six authors. They trace the disagreement to the meta-analysis, which *"suffers from shortcomings with respect to sample selection and analytical coherence"*, and conclude that a corrected assessment *"suggests that research on climate and conflict to date has produced mixed and inconclusive results."*

**Simulator recommendation: encode the climate→conflict edge with an explicit uncertainty flag and a tunable multiplier spanning [≈1.0, 1.14 per σ for intergroup conflict], with the low end representing the Buhaug et al. position that the effect may not be robustly identified at all.** A model that hard-codes 14%/σ is taking a side in a live dispute; a model that offers the range is more honest and more interesting to play.

## 5. Climate tipping-element interactions — the one literature with real, published edge multipliers

**Wunderling N, Donges JF, Kurths J, Winkelmann R (2021). "Interacting tipping elements increase risk of climate domino effects under global warming." *Earth System Dynamics* 12: 601–619. https://doi.org/10.5194/esd-12-601-2021** (CC-BY; received 26 March 2020, published 3 June 2021). I read the PDF directly, including Tables 1–3 and §3.1–3.3, because the first automated summary of this paper mangled several numbers.

**Their Table 2 gives coupling strengths in exactly the form a simulator needs, and the caption states the semantics verbatim:** *"the strength s_ij gives an estimate in terms of increased or decreased probability of cascading transitions (Kriegler et al., 2009). For example, if Greenland transgresses its threshold, the probability that the AMOC does as well is increased by a factor of 10… Conversely, the probability that Greenland transgresses its threshold if the AMOC is in the transitioned state is decreased by a factor of 1/10."* **These are literally conditional probability multipliers on an adjacency list.**

| Link | Max s_ij (a.u.) | Physical process |
|---|---|---|
| Greenland → AMOC | **+10** | Freshwater influx |
| AMOC → Greenland | **−10** | Reduction in northward heat transport |
| Greenland → West Antarctica | **+10** | Sea level rise |
| AMOC → Amazon rainforest | **±2 up to ±4** (sign unclear) | Changes in precipitation patterns |
| West Antarctica → AMOC | **±3** (sign unclear) | Increase in meridional salinity gradient (−); fast advection of freshwater anomaly to North Atlantic (+) |
| West Antarctica → Greenland | **+2** | Sea level rise |
| AMOC → West Antarctica | **+1.5** | Heat accumulation in Southern Ocean |

Their Table 1 critical global-mean-temperature ranges (from Schellnhuber et al. 2016): **Greenland 0.8–3.2 °C; West Antarctica 0.8–5.5 °C; AMOC 3.5–6.0 °C; Amazon rainforest 3.5–4.5 °C.** Intrinsic transition timescales calibrated at 4 °C reference warming: **Greenland 4900 y, West Antarctica 2400 y, AMOC 300 y, Amazon 50 y** — a four-order-of-magnitude spread that a turn-based simulator must respect or it will produce nonsense.

**Aggregate results, verbatim from §3.1–3.3** (Monte Carlo ensemble of ~3.7 million members):

- *"the ensemble average of the critical temperature at an interaction strength of d = 1.0 is lowered by about 1.2 °C (≈ 40 %) for the West Antarctic Ice Sheet, 2.75 °C (≈ 55 %) for the AMOC and 0.5 °C (≈ 10 %) for the Amazon rainforest… In contrast, the critical temperature range for the Greenland Ice Sheet tends to be raised"* (because of the strong negative Greenland↔AMOC feedback loop).
- *"For global warming up to 2.0 °C, tipping occurs in 61 % of all simulations. This comprises the tipping of individual elements (22 %) as well as cascades including two elements (21 %), three elements (15 %) and four elements (3 %)."* Their Table 3 shows this **61% total is insensitive to maximum interaction strength (identical at d = 1.0, 0.75, 0.50, 0.25, 0.10) — but the cascade-size distribution is not:** single-element events rise from 22% to 56% and three-element cascades fall from 15% to 0% as d drops from 1.0 to 0.10. **Coupling strength changes the shape of the disaster, not its frequency.** That is a superb design principle for a simulator: interaction terms should redistribute severity, not merely inflate the event rate.
- *"in up to 65 % of all ensemble simulations, the Greenland Ice Sheet triggers tipping cascades"*; the polar ice sheets are the **initiators**, the AMOC is the **mediator/transmitter**; cascades between the ice sheets and/or the AMOC sum to *"up to 80 % of all tipping cascades with two or three elements."*
- *"Tipping cascades are first induced at warming levels around 1 °C… The bulk of tipping cascades, however, is found between a 1 and 3 °C GMT increase… For temperatures above a 3 °C GMT increase, cascades occur less frequently as most of the tipping elements already transgress their individual thresholds before this temperature."* **The cascade hazard is non-monotonic in warming.** A naive "hotter = more cascades" curve is wrong.
- Their own honesty flag, verbatim: *"Our approach is conservative in the sense that there are several destabilising interactions that are not considered here."* And they position the work as *"a hypotheses generator that produces qualitative scenarios (rather than exact quantifications or projections)."* **Encode these multipliers, but label them elicitation-derived (Kriegler et al. 2009) and conceptual-model-propagated, not observed.**

## 6. Other quantified couplings retrieved

**Nuclear war → global famine.** Xia L, Robock A, Scherrer K, Harrison CS, Bodirsky BL, Weindl I, Jägermeyr J, Bardeen CG, Toon OB, Heneghan R (2022). "Global food insecurity and famine from reduced crop, marine fishery and livestock production due to climate disruption from nuclear war soot injection." *Nature Food* 3: 586–596. https://doi.org/10.1038/s43016-022-00573-0. PMID 37118594. Verbatim abstract: *"Soot injections larger than 5 Tg would lead to mass food shortages, and livestock and aquatic food production would be unable to compensate for reduced crop output, in almost all countries. More than 2 billion people could die from nuclear war between India and Pakistan, and more than 5 billion could die from a war between the United States and Russia."* Their earlier preprint (Research Square, DOI 10.21203/rs.3.rs-830419/v1) states: *"famine would result for most of Earth even from a war between India and Pakistan using less than 3% of the global nuclear arsenal."* **This is the largest severity multiplier in the entire literature: the indirect (climate → agriculture → famine) death toll exceeds the direct blast toll by roughly an order of magnitude.** Any simulator that models nuclear war as a blast-casualty event is off by ~10×.

**Base rates (all from Cotton-Barratt et al. 2016, read directly):**
- Supervolcanic eruption: *"estimated to occur very approximately every 30,000 years"* (noting *"which may or may not cause a global catastrophe"* — an event-probability, not a catastrophe-probability).
- Asteroid impact large enough to cause a global catastrophe: *"estimated every 120,000 years."*
- Asteroid encounter that *"might cause a globally significant effect"*: *"approximately 1 in 1,250"* in a 100-year period (they attribute this to Reinhardt et al.). Annual probability-weighted expected cost of all NEOs as of 2010: **91 fatalities/year.**
- Asteroid severity scaling, quoting a US National Research Council report verbatim: *"above the conservatively assumed global catastrophe threshold from a 1.5km-diameter impactor, the number of fatalities ramps up from 10% of the world's population to the entire population for impactors above 10km in diameter."*
- Lab-origin pandemic, quoting Lipsitch & Inglesby verbatim: *"work with a novel, transmissible form of influenza virus carries a risk of between 0.01% and 0.1% per laboratory-year of creating a pandemic… or between 0.05% and 0.6% per full-time worker-year."*
- Historical anchors: Plague of Justinian killed *"between 13% and 17% of the world population at the time"*; the 1918–1920 influenza pandemic killed *"50 - 100 million people – between 2.5% and 5% of the world population"*; ~1% of world population died in WWI, up to 3% in WWII.
- Their Figure 2.7 places **natural pandemic, nuclear war and engineered pandemic** in the higher-likelihood/high-attention quadrant over the next 5 years, and **catastrophic climate change, catastrophic disruption from AI, failure of geo-engineering** in the lower-likelihood/high-attention quadrant, with **asteroid impact and supervolcanic eruption** lower-likelihood/low-attention.

One retrieved third-party figure of the same family, from Cotton-Barratt et al. 2016: *"The UK's Stern Review on the Economics of Climate Change suggested a 0.1% chance of human extinction each year"*, which *"would imply a 9.5% chance of human extinction within the next hundred years."*

**Keeping (a)/(b)/(c) apart.** Three quantities are routinely conflated and must be separate columns in the simulator's data model:
- **(a) P(event)** — e.g. supervolcano ~1/30,000 yr; asteroid globally-significant-effect 1-in-1,250 per century; GoF-lab pandemic 0.01–0.1% per lab-year.
- **(b) P(kills a large fraction | event)** — e.g. Xia et al.'s >2bn / >5bn conditional on specific soot injections; the NRC's 10%→100% fatality ramp conditional on impactor diameter. Note the GCF's own careful hedge that a supervolcanic eruption *"may or may not cause a global catastrophe"* — the (a)→(b) step is where most published rates silently smuggle in an assumption.
- **(c) P(permanent extinction)** — Kemp et al.'s *"probability of human extinction within a given timeframe"*; Ord's 1-in-6; the Stern-derived 9.5%/century. **Nothing in the cascade literature reviewed here bears on (c) at all.** Every historical cascade in §4 is a (b)-type event at regional scale. Do not let a cascade model's outputs be read as extinction probabilities.

## 8. Six design recommendations that fall out of the evidence

1. **Two state variables per system, multiplicatively combined.** Lawrence et al.'s core model is stress × trigger, with the trigger's impact *multiplied* by accumulated stress. Independent Bernoulli draws per hazard cannot produce this.
2. **Route hazards through a shared critical-systems layer** (Avin et al.). Correlated failure then emerges rather than being hand-tuned.
3. **Make interaction strength change the cascade-size distribution, not the event rate.** Wunderling et al. found the total tipping fraction pinned at 61% across a 10× range of coupling strength while the size distribution moved enormously. That is the signature of a well-formed coupling parameter.
4. **Give damage functions a policy/institution modulator.** This recurs independently in Laki (healthcare availability), the Victorian famines (relief and export policy), and food riots (regime type). The physical shock sets the *possibility*; the institutional node sets the *conversion rate*.
5. **Model super-additive interaction terms explicitly.** Moscow 2010 is the existence proof: 18% of deaths came from a term that neither hazard alone produces. If your damage model is additive over concurrent hazards, you are structurally understating compound events.
6. **Keep three separate probability columns** — P(event), P(mass casualties | event), P(extinction) — and refuse to let cascade dynamics write into the third. Nothing in this literature licenses that inference.
